How solar panels got cheap

By Jordan Rivers

Twenty years ago a solar panel was an expensive curiosity found on calculators and satellites. Today it is the cheapest way to make new electricity in most of the world. The story of how that happened is mostly a story about factories, not physics.

Learning by making

Every time the world doubled the number of panels it had ever built, the price of a panel fell by roughly a fifth. Engineers call this a learning curve. It shows up because each new factory is a little smarter than the last one, with thinner wafers, faster saws and fewer broken cells.

Policy mattered too. Guaranteed prices for solar power in a few countries created a market large enough for manufacturers to build at a scale nobody had tried before. Once the scale existed, costs fell everywhere, including in places that never paid those prices.

The panel did not change much. The way we make it changed completely.

What comes next is less about the panels and more about everything around them: storage, grids and the permits needed to connect a new farm. Those parts have not followed the same curve yet. That is where the next decade of progress will have to come from.
